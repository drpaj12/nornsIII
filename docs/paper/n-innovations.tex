\section{The Innovations}
\label{sec:innovations}

Each subsection gives the idea in plain English first and the technical description second. Which experiments support each innovation is in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{A packer that keeps the HDL's registers together}
\label{sec:i-packer}

\pp{Plain English.} Before placement, a tool packs small pieces of logic into tile-sized groups. The standard packer chooses pieces that share wires. It does not know that eight flip-flops are the eight bits of one register, so it can scatter them over many tiles even though they will be read and written together. Our packer is a copy of the standard one that adds a bonus for putting pieces of the same register, bus or module into the same tile.

\pp{Technical.} The Norns packer is a patch to VPR's AAPack clusterer (VTR commit \code{3c9a4d23b2}, 8 changed files, 536 added lines; \code{patches/}). Each atom carries a group id read from the affinity file (\code{--norns\_affinity}); the group is the module, the word, or the word with io-words (source \emph{module}, \emph{word}, \emph{word+io}). A CLB's dominant group is the plurality group of its atoms so far. A candidate molecule gains $+\beta$ (\code{--norns\_affinity\_weight}) for every atom in that group, and up to 30 unclustered members of the group are proposed as candidates, because the bits of a word are parallel and no net links them. The stock gain lies in $[0,1]$, so $\beta$ is in the units of one connected atom. Legality stays with AAPack's legaliser. With the option absent or $\beta=0$ the packer must write a \code{.net} byte-identical to stock; this was checked on 60 comparisons over 15 designs before any experiment. Words come from Odin~III's provenance record; io-words extend a top-level bus inward over LUT and flip-flop atoms (two hops past the atom next to the pad, through nets of fanout at most 16).

\subsection{A yardstick with a known best grouping}
\label{sec:i-yardstick}

\pp{Plain English.} To know whether a packer is good we need designs where the best grouping is known, so that the packer can be scored against it. We have two such yardsticks. The first (this subsection) is synthetic: we build designs out of blocks that each exactly fill one tile, wired as chains, meshes, trees or random graphs. Because every block fills one tile, no packer can use fewer tiles than there are blocks, and the block grouping reaches that count; the tile count of the best grouping is therefore known exactly. That is the whole claim. It is not a proven best placement, not a proven best wire length and not a proven best speed: the known grouping is handed to the same placer and router as every other grouping, and what comes out is what that tool achieves from it. A packer may even beat it on wire or speed. The same logic can be written in the HDL with its registers lined up with the blocks, or deliberately misaligned, and that turns out to matter a great deal (Section~\ref{sec:i-leak}). The second yardstick, built from real designs, follows in Section~\ref{sec:i-yardstick-real}.

\pp{Why the synthetic count is exact.} A block is ten six-input functions, each feeding one flip-flop. A six-input function needs a whole logic element of the architecture (the two-LUT mode shares five input pins and takes five-input functions only), and the flip-flop can only be fed by its own function, so the ten functions need ten logic elements, one CLB. A design of $B$ blocks therefore needs at least $B$ CLBs in every packing, whatever the packer, and the block partition uses exactly $B$. This is a counting argument and does not depend on the packer. It does not cover wire length or delay, and it does not prove that the designed cut (the nets that leave a block, a few per block) is the smallest possible.

\pp{Technical.} A block is 10 six-input functions, each feeding a flip-flop (ten logic elements of the \code{k6\_frac\_N10} architecture, so exactly one CLB), with dense internal connections and at most two external inputs per function; the CLB count $B$ is an exact lower bound reached by the block partition. A generator (\code{gen/r5gen.py}, seed 20261026) writes 72 designs: four topologies (chain, mesh, tree, random sparse), nine sizes $B=4$ to $1024$, two styles (\code{dp}: a datapath slice whose HDL has the vector \code{r[9:0]}; \code{ctl}: scalar registers), at two levels (NL: the netlist written directly as BLIF; HDL: the same specification as Verilog through Odin~III). At the netlist level the check also requires that swapping any two logic elements of different blocks never lowers the cut. The \emph{known} arm hands the block partition to the Norns packer as affinity groups with $\beta=100$; the ratios in this paper are against the placement VPR produces from that clustering, so they are a yardstick and not a bound. A second generator (\code{gen/r6gen.py}) fixes the logic and varies only the HDL declarations: at alignment $a$ a fraction $1-a$ of each block's flip-flops is displaced into other words, at $a\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ for mesh, tree and chain with seven sizes (105 designs). The idea follows Cong, Romesis and Xie~\cite{Cong03tpeko}.

\pp{What it found, and what it cannot claim.} The standard packer uses 1.073 times the CLBs, 1.220 times the wire and 1.093 times the delay of the known grouping over the 72 designs, and 1.489 times the wire on meshes and trees (Section~\ref{sec:e-room}). The synthetic designs are regular by construction: every block is the same size, the wiring between blocks is a fixed pattern, and, as Section~\ref{sec:i-rent} measures, they have almost no Rent growth. A regular design may hide the true gap, in either direction, and the 1.489 for meshes and trees against 1.0 for chains and random graphs shows that the gap depends on the pattern. This is why the second yardstick starts from real designs.

\subsection{A yardstick built from real designs}
\label{sec:i-yardstick-real}

\pp{Plain English.} Take a real design and a grouping of its logic into tiles (what VPR's own packer made, or what an independent partitioner made). Then edit the design, touching only the inside of each group, until that grouping is provably the best one for tile count and a strict local optimum for wiring between groups. The wiring between groups, which is the real design's own structure, stays as it was. We then hand the edited design to the standard packer and to our packer told the grouping, and compare. Three edits do the work. (1) Pad every group with dummy logic until it is exactly full, so no packer can use fewer tiles than there are groups. (2) Make every look-up table use all six inputs, so two small tables cannot share one logic element. (3) Add connections inside each group, never between groups, so that moving any single logic cell out of its group makes the number of cut nets rise. The idea is that of Cong, Romesis and Xie~\cite{Cong03tpeko}; we apply it to real structure because the synthetic yardstick is too regular (Section~\ref{sec:i-rent}).

\pp{Technical.} The design $D$ is the Odin~III netlist after VPR's own clean-up. A clustering $C_0$ is either \emph{own} (the clusters of VPR's standard packer, seed 1) or \emph{foreign} (METIS $k$-way on the cell hypergraph, the lowest cut of 40 seeds followed by pair-swap descent, with $K$ the number of own clusters). It is repaired for six-input accounting (a cluster of fourteen fractured small LUTs now needs fourteen logic elements) into $C$ with $K$ clusters, so $K$ can exceed VPR's own cluster count; clusters respect at most 32 external input nets and 12 external output nets, which the default flow can form, and carry chains are cut into segments of at most 12 adders (one carry net removed per boundary, counted). Each cluster is padded with dummy cells (a six-input LUT feeding a flip-flop) to exactly 10 logic elements, with dummy inputs only from nets driven inside the cluster. The CLB bound is then exact by counting: a six-input cell cannot share a logic element, a flip-flop is fed only by its own LUT, hard blocks are other block types, and a CLB has 10 logic elements, so every legal packing needs at least $10K/10=K$ CLBs and $C$ reaches $K$. The inside is strengthened by filling every LUT to six inputs from nets driven inside its cluster, doubling a net that has a single pin in a cluster, and anchoring each cell to a dummy's output. No net that leaves a cluster is added or rewired, except that one pad or hard-block input of a full cell may be replaced by a dummy output (counted).

\pp{Margin, check and shuffles.} Among the partitions with the minimum number of CLBs every part holds exactly 10 logic elements, and for those the construction proves a swap margin: swapping any two cells of different clusters raises the cut by at least the margin computed from the shared nets (the measured minimum is 1 or 2 on every pilot build), so $C$ is a strict local optimum of the single-swap neighbourhood when no cell is left unanchored. A cell with no spare input slot and no dummy to read cannot be strengthened without rewiring real nets; such cells are counted as unmet and flag the design (0 to 3 cells in four of the six pilot builds, 14 in the foreign build of diffeq1). The optimum is not proved global. As a check, 100 METIS runs (repaired to exactly full parts and refined by pair-swap search) and 100 restarts of a swap search from $C$ with 10\% of the cells perturbed were run per design; none found a smaller cut. For diffeq1 no METIS partition satisfied the pin limits, so that check is vacuous there and the search from $C$ is the evidence. The result is evidence and not proof. To remove file order as a clue the BLIF atom and net names are replaced by random tokens and the statement order is permuted, three shuffles per design, plus an unshuffled \emph{ordered} control. To expose self-bias, the foreign construction asks whether VPR does relatively better on a grouping that is its own answer than on one that is not.

\pp{What it can and cannot claim.} Area is exact (CLB count, by counting). The cut is optimal by margin against single swaps and checked, not proved, against larger rearrangements. The critical-path delay is observed and not claimed: the known arm's delay is what VPR achieves from $C$, and ratios below 1 are possible. Hard blocks and pads are outside the claim (each net between them and logic is cut in every grouping and is a constant). The pilot and its full-set slot are in Section~\ref{sec:e-real}.

\subsection{Why the synthetic yardstick is regular: Rent's rule}
\label{sec:i-rent}

\pp{Plain English.} Real circuits get more connected to the rest of the chip as a block grows, at a rate that Rent's rule describes by one number, the Rent exponent. A synthetic design made from identical blocks with a fixed handful of wires between them has hardly any such growth. If the packer's gap is measured only on such designs, we cannot tell how big the gap is on real logic. We measured it.

\pp{Technical.} The exponent is the Mimir metric \code{rent.p} (recursive bisection with METIS, three partitioner seeds), read from the Mimir main store without modification and split by design set by \code{scripts/rent\_split.py} (output \code{data/analysis/rent-split.json}). The synthetic designs have fixed numbers of external inputs and outputs, and their nets stay inside blocks or follow the block pattern.

\subsection{A leak found by the yardstick}
\label{sec:i-leak}

\pp{Plain English.} In our first test of the packer on the yardstick designs, the packer used about ten percent less wire than the standard packer. That looked like a result. It was not. In those designs the file that tells the packer which pieces belong together was written from the same blocks that define the best grouping, so the packer was handed the answer. Removing the answer and giving the packer registers that cut across the blocks, the packer used between 65\% and 150\% \emph{more} wire. We report this as a finding about method: always check what the tool is given, and build the yardstick so that the hint can be dialled from perfect to useless.

\pp{Technical.} The \emph{leak} is word-file purity against the blocks near 1: 0.9998 in the \code{dp} designs, and the original gain over 63 designs came only from the 18 mesh and tree \code{dp} designs among them (docs/plan/round8-h22.md). The alignment ladder of the previous subsection varies the purity from 1 to 0.1. The H22$'$ re-run replaces the HDL register declarations by word buses of width $w\in\{4,8,16,32\}$ over a random permutation of the flip-flops (no width is a multiple of 10, so words cut across blocks), for mesh and tree (test) and chain (control), seven sizes each: 84 designs, arms stock, norns ($\beta=4$) and known, three seeds (\code{gen/r8gen.py}).

\subsection{What the standard packer keeps together on slow paths}
\label{sec:i-census}

\pp{Plain English.} A direct count of which kinds of pairs of logic pieces the standard packer puts in one tile: pairs on a slow path in series, pairs that share an input, and pairs that merely fill space. We also ask whether the connections that are almost as slow as the slowest are kept inside tiles more often than average connections.

\pp{Technical.} A log-only census in the packer (\code{--norns\_pack\_census}) records, for every molecule added to a CLB, the candidate source and the gain terms. Every pair of LUT, flip-flop and adder atoms in one CLB falls in the first applicable pairing class: series-critical (a local net of fanout at most 32 from one to the other, pack-time sink criticality at least 0.5), series-noncritical, shared-input, orthogonal (same word, or sinks of one broadcast net), filler. Fifty random packings with the same CLB sizes give the chance level. Post-route criticality comes from Odin~III's critical-path attribution of the routed runs. Near-critical connections are those within 10\% of the CPD in the routed report.

\subsection{Starting placement from the designer's grouping (a negative baseline)}
\label{sec:i-seed}

\pp{Plain English.} The simplest use of the designer's grouping is to give the placer a rough starting layout that keeps the groups together, and then let the heated search refine it. We ask how hot the search may start before it throws that layout away. This was the first idea of the Norns work. Its experiments are negative, and it is kept as the baseline that the packer is compared against in spirit.

\pp{Technical.} A seed is a partition of the placed blocks: the provenance hierarchy (Urd) or a Loki~III L2 clustering (Verdandi: Louvain, spectral, affinity propagation, $k$-means at $k$, $2k$, $4k$ with $k$ the module count). The quotient layout puts clusters on the die; each cluster takes the free CLB slots nearest its laid-out point. A soft seed enters VPR as an initial placement file (\code{--read\_initial\_place\_file}); a hard seed as one \code{PartitionRegion} per cluster (\code{--read\_vpr\_constraints}); pads and multiplier tiles stay anchored and carry chains move as one vertical run. The crossover sweep cold-starts the anneal from $P^*$, the best final placement over all seeds, raises the scale $S$ and finds the crossover; all seeds are then run at $2S$ against an unseeded run at the same temperature (Figure~\ref{fig:norns}).

\subsection{Outside placers and run records}
\label{sec:i-tools}

\pp{Plain English.} To tell whether a finding belongs to VPR or to placement in general, we run a placer from outside the VTR tools, feed its result back into VPR, and let VPR route and time it, so every placer is judged by the same router and timer. Every run writes a record of what was run and where each parameter came from, so M\'{\i}mir can store it.

\pp{Technical.} A converter writes the outside placement (nextpnr HeAP or SA) in the flat-placement file format (\code{--read\_flat\_place}); a reread control reads VPR's own final placement back, and each ratio is within a family (analytic with analytic, annealing with annealing). Rule H0 fixes adders, multipliers, RAMs and pads at VPR's analytic positions, so the outside placers inherit part of VPR's solution and nextpnr has no timing model (both biases are in every table caption). Each run writes a \code{mimir-run.json} (\code{scripts/runrecord.py}).
