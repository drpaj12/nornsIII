\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An FPGA is a chip made of many small programmable logic blocks joined by programmable wires; a design is loaded onto it by setting those parts. A designer writes the design in a hardware description language (HDL), and a chain of CAD programs turns that text into a pattern on the chip. Two steps of the chain decide most of the result. Packing groups small pieces of logic into tile-sized groups, and placement decides where on the chip each group sits. Packing and placement see the design as a long list of gates and wires. The grouping the designer wrote down (this register, that adder, that block of the source) is mostly gone by then.

This paper asks one question: does keeping the designer's grouping, or any other cheap change to packing and placement, make the result better, and how can we find out honestly? The answer is a closed negative one. We answer it with hypotheses. Each hypothesis is stated in plain language, an experiment is run, and the experiment either confirms it or does not. Ideas that the experiments confirm are in the main text; ideas that failed are one line in Table~\ref{tab:notwork}, with their records in Appendix~\ref{sec:records}. The work ends here, with a negative-results paper whose lasting contribution is a set of designs with a known best grouping, because the experiments that followed it found no change that beats noise.

\pp{What good means.} A real design is judged on three things at once: the chip area it needs (low), its speed (high) and its power (low). We measure area and speed. Power is not measured in any experiment of this paper. Speed means the slowest path of the design and also the paths nearly as slow, because speeding up the slowest path alone only promotes the next one (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\pp{The data behind it.} M\'{\i}mir~III is the dataset this work feeds. Every piece of the circuit carries a record of the HDL text it came from. M\'{\i}mir stores that record beside where each piece was placed and how fast its paths are, so the data can show \emph{why} things ended up where they did, and not only where. Section~\ref{sec:mimir} says what M\'{\i}mir holds today and what it lacks.

\pp{Headline results.} Each is given in plain language here, with the numbers and the register verdicts in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\textbf{Room.} The tool for finding out is a \emph{known-optimum yardstick}: a design built from blocks that each fill exactly one tile, so the best tile grouping is known by construction. Against it the standard packer leaves room. It uses 22\% more wire over 72 generated designs and 49\% more on meshes and trees, and on 33 real designs made into yardsticks it needs 1.103 times the proven lower bound on tiles (corrected bound) and 1.265 times the wire, with the delay inconclusive (Section~\ref{sec:r-room}).

\textbf{Where the loss is.} 60\% of the tile gap is one kind of pick: an atom of another known group taken while the right one was queued. An oracle that picks the first atom of each tile with the answer key closes 11\% of the gap, and no feature computed from the design alone ranks the right atom well once the construction is removed (Section~\ref{sec:r-loses}).

\textbf{The packer that keeps the registers.} It keeps them together at no cost in tiles, and when the registers equal the best blocks it uses 0.669 of the standard wire. That gain was a \emph{leak}: the input file already held the answer. The yardstick found it. With registers that cut across the blocks the packer used 65\% to 150\% more wire (Section~\ref{sec:r-packer}).

\textbf{Nothing deployable beat noise.} Refreshing the timing weights while packing, a rule for the first atom of each tile, a two-hop gain term, spare room in critical groups and duplication after placement each moved the mean delay by between $-2.1\%$ and $+0.7\%$, with wins and losses nearly even (best: 17 wins, 14 losses, 4 ties). The two seeming speed gains were selections on the scored number. Starting placement from the designer's grouping, re-grouping after seeing the placement and outside placers did not help either (Section~\ref{sec:summary}, Table~\ref{tab:notwork}).

\pp{What the paper holds.} Sections~\ref{sec:background} to~\ref{sec:innovations} define the terms, the method and the instruments. Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the results as they stand and what did not work. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} gives the reading and the limits, and Section~\ref{sec:future} the ten untested ideas. Appendix~\ref{sec:a-instruments} describes the instruments of the earlier rounds, Appendix~\ref{sec:records} holds one record for each round or question in time order (including the known-optimal grouping idea of Cong et al., Appendix~\ref{sec:e-tpeko}), and Appendix~\ref{sec:log} is the research register. The register has 88 rows: 15 \textsc{useful}, 10 \textsc{possibly good}, 27 \textsc{inconclusive}, 25 \textsc{dismissed} and eleven measurements; ten untested ideas sit beside them.
